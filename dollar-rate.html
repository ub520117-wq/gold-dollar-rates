Interbank rate vs open market rate

There are two dollar rates in Pakistan, and the gap between them matters. The interbank rate is the rate banks charge each other, published by the State Bank of Pakistan. It is the benchmark that news reports quote and the figure shown on this page. The open market rate is what exchange companies and money changers offer the public for cash — usually a little higher when you buy dollars and a little lower when you sell them, because the dealer takes a spread.

For most people the practical rate is the one they actually get. If you are sending remittances home, compare two or three services: the headline rate is only half the story, and the fee and the delivery time make up the rest. A service advertising a slightly worse rate but no fee can easily beat a service with a better rate and a heavy charge.

Why the dollar rate moves

The rupee's value against the dollar is set by supply and demand. When Pakistan earns more dollars than it spends — through exports, remittances from overseas Pakistanis, and foreign investment — the rupee strengthens. When imports, debt repayments and other outflows exceed inflows, the rupee weakens. The State Bank holds dollar reserves and can smooth sharp moves, but it cannot hold a rate against the market indefinitely.

Two forces dominate the headlines: the trade balance and remittances. Pakistan imports far more than it exports, which creates steady demand for dollars. Remittances from the millions of Pakistanis working in the Gulf, the UK, the US and Europe are the largest single source of dollars coming in — which is why months with strong remittance inflows tend to be kinder to the rupee.
