Gold investment guide for Pakistan

Bars, coins, jewellery or funds — the honest trade-offs of each, and what to check before you buy.

Gold is Pakistan's oldest savings habit. It needs no bank account, no paperwork and no trust in any institution — just a safe place to keep it. That is a real advantage in a country where the rupee has lost value against the dollar over long periods. But gold is not a magic investment, and the form you buy it in matters as much as the decision to buy it.

Why Pakistanis buy gold

Two reasons dominate. The first is protection: when the rupee weakens, the rupee price of gold rises, because gold is priced in dollars worldwide. Holding gold is, in effect, holding dollars in metal form. The second is culture: gold is the traditional gift at weddings, the standard store of family wealth, and something that can be sold quickly in any town in the country. Both reasons are legitimate. The mistake is treating gold as a growth investment: over long periods, gold has preserved purchasing power reasonably well, but it has not matched the returns of productive assets, and it pays no income while you hold it.

The four ways to own gold

Gold bars and coins are the purest investment form. You pay close to the metal value plus a small premium, and you can sell them back close to the metal value. Ten-tola bars and one-tola coins are the common sizes. The catch is storage and security: you need a safe place, and a bank locker costs money each year.

Jewellery is the most popular form and the worst investment form. You pay making charges of a significant percentage on top of the metal value, and you lose those charges when you sell. Jewellery is for wearing and for weddings; if investment is the goal, bars are cheaper.

Gold funds and ETFs let you own gold without holding metal, through a fund that tracks the price. They solve the storage problem and can be bought and sold in small amounts, but they need a brokerage account, charge an annual fee, and are not available to everyone in Pakistan.

Digital gold — apps and platforms that sell fractional gold — is growing, but it is the least regulated corner of the market. Check carefully who holds the metal, how you redeem it, and what fees apply before trusting an app with your savings.

The costs nobody mentions

Every way of owning gold has a cost: the buy-sell spread (the gap between what you pay and what you get back), making charges on jewellery, locker rent, fund fees, and the risk of theft or fraud. Add them up before you buy. A bar bought at a 2% premium and sold at a 1% discount needs the price to rise 3% just for you to break even. That is a fair price for liquidity and safety — but it is a cost, and pretending it does not exist is how people lose money on "safe" gold.

How much gold is enough

Financial advisers who recommend gold usually suggest a modest share of a portfolio — the oft-quoted range is 5 to 10%. The reasoning is diversification: gold tends to do well when other things do badly, so a small holding smooths the ride. A portfolio that is mostly gold is not diversified at all — it is a bet on one metal. In Pakistan, where many families already hold a large share of their wealth in gold jewellery, the honest answer is often that they already own enough, and the next rupee is better placed elsewhere.

Before you buy: a checklist

Buy from a jeweller you can find again next year. Check the hallmark and the weight on the scale in front of you. Get the karat, the weight, the metal price and the making charges written separately on the receipt. Compare the per-tola metal price with the live rate on this site before you pay. And keep the receipt — it is what proves the purchase if you ever sell or insure the piece.
