Why the dollar rate changes in Pakistan

Supply and demand, remittances, reserves and the State Bank — what actually moves USD/PKR.

The dollar rate is the most watched number in Pakistan's economy, and the most misunderstood. It is not set by any one person or office. It is the price of a dollar in rupees, set by supply and demand in the interbank market, where banks buy and sell currencies with each other all day. When more people want dollars than want to sell them, the price rises. When dollars are plentiful, it falls.

Supply and demand, in plain words

Think of dollars as a good that Pakistan both earns and spends. The country earns dollars by exporting goods (textiles, rice, leather, sports goods), by selling services abroad, by receiving remittances from Pakistanis working overseas, and by attracting foreign investment and loans. It spends dollars on imports (oil, machinery, food, medicines), on repaying foreign debt, and on Pakistanis travelling, studying and investing abroad. If outflows exceed inflows, dollars become scarce and the rupee falls. If inflows exceed outflows, the rupee strengthens. That is the whole story; everything else is detail.

The trade balance

Pakistan imports much more than it exports, which creates a permanent underlying demand for dollars. Oil is the largest single import, so the world oil price feeds directly into the dollar rate: when oil is expensive, Pakistan needs more dollars to buy it, and the rupee comes under pressure. Textile exports are the largest earner, which is why a good cotton crop and competitive export prices help the rupee.

Remittances: the biggest inflow

Remittances from overseas Pakistanis are the largest and steadiest source of dollars coming into the country — larger than exports in many years. Millions of Pakistanis work in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, the UK, the US and Europe, and the money they send home pays for their families' food, housing, education and weddings. Months with strong remittance inflows — typically around Eid and holiday seasons — tend to be kinder to the rupee. Anything that affects overseas employment, from Gulf construction booms to visa policy changes, eventually shows up in the exchange rate.

Reserves and the State Bank

The State Bank of Pakistan holds foreign exchange reserves — a stockpile of dollars — and can sell dollars into the market to slow a sharp fall, or buy them to build reserves when the rupee is strong. But reserves are finite, and a central bank cannot defend a rate against the market forever. When reserves are low, the rupee is more vulnerable to every shock; when reserves are healthy, the market is calmer. News about IMF programmes, foreign loans and debt repayments moves the rate largely because it changes expectations about reserves.

The dollar itself moves too

Sometimes the rupee falls even when nothing changed in Pakistan — because the dollar rose everywhere. When the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, money flows into dollar assets from around the world, and almost every currency weakens against the dollar. When the Fed cuts rates, the pressure eases. So the USD/PKR rate has two stories in it: Pakistan's story, and the dollar's story.

What it means for you

For most people, the rate matters in three places: remittances (how many rupees arrive for each dollar sent), imported prices (fuel, cooking oil, medicines and machinery all get dearer when the rupee falls), and savings (gold and dollars are the two hedges Pakistanis have traditionally used against a falling rupee). Nobody can predict the rate reliably — anyone who claims to be sure is selling something. What you can do is understand the forces, avoid panic buying or selling on a single day's news, and compare rates and fees whenever you actually convert money.
