Dollar vs rupee in 2026: what is driving the rate

The forces shaping the rupee this year — trade, remittances, reserves and the global dollar.

Every year the rupee's story is written by the same four forces: what Pakistan sells, what it buys, what overseas Pakistanis send home, and how much the State Bank holds in reserve. 2026 is no exception. This article explains those forces and what to watch — without pretending anyone can predict the rate.

The trade gap sets the background

Pakistan imports more than it exports, and the gap has to be paid for in dollars. Oil is the biggest import bill, followed by machinery, food and medicines. When world oil prices rise, the import bill rises with them, and the demand for dollars rises too. On the other side, textile exports remain the largest earner, and their competitiveness depends on energy prices, the cotton crop and demand in Europe and America. A year in which exports grow faster than imports is a year the rupee breathes more easily; a year in which the gap widens is a year of pressure.

Remittances are the counterweight

Remittances from the millions of Pakistanis working abroad — in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, the UK, the US and Europe — are the largest single inflow of dollars into the country, and the steadiest. They rise around Eid and the holiday seasons, and they rise when overseas employment is strong. Because they are sent by families rather than investors, they do not flee at the first sign of trouble the way portfolio investment can. That steadiness is the rupee's best friend.

Reserves and confidence

The State Bank's foreign exchange reserves are the buffer. When reserves are comfortable, the market is calm: importers can get dollars, debt payments are covered, and speculation stays muted. When reserves run low, every piece of news moves the rate, because traders know the buffer is thin. News about IMF programmes, loans from friendly countries and debt repayments matters mostly through this channel — it changes expectations about reserves, and expectations move markets before the money itself moves.

The global dollar

The fourth force has nothing to do with Pakistan. When the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, the dollar strengthens against nearly every currency in the world, and the rupee weakens along with them. When the Fed cuts rates, the pressure eases. So part of any move in USD/PKR is a global story, not a Pakistani one — and judging the rupee only against the dollar can mislead. A useful habit is to check the rupee against a basket — the euro, the pound and gold — before deciding whether the rupee itself moved or the dollar did.

What to watch, and what to ignore

Watch the monthly trade numbers, remittance inflows and reserves — they are the fundamentals, and they are published regularly. Ignore daily noise and social-media predictions: a single day's move says nothing about next month. And if you need to convert money, the rate you get matters more than the rate on the news — compare the total cost, fee included, across two or three services before you send anything.
