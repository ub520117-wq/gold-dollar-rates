Is gold a good investment in Pakistan?

What gold has actually done for savers, where it disappoints, and how much of a portfolio it deserves.

Ask ten Pakistanis where to keep savings safe and eight will say gold. It is the country's oldest answer to a weak rupee, and it has often been the right one. But "often right" is not "always right", and gold's record deserves a closer look than the bazaar gives it.

What gold actually does

Gold's job is preservation, not growth. An ounce of gold buys roughly the same basket of goods today that it bought a generation ago — that is the point. It holds its value across decades and across currency crises, which is exactly what a saver in a country with a depreciating currency needs. When the rupee falls, gold priced in rupees rises, so a saver who held gold instead of rupees kept their purchasing power. That is a genuine achievement, and it explains gold's reputation.

But preservation is a low bar. Gold pays no dividend, no interest and no rent. A rupee in gold today is a rupee that earns nothing until the price rises — and the price can fall for years at a time. Anyone who bought gold at a peak and needed the money two years later learned that the hard way. Gold's long-run return has been positive in rupee terms, largely because the rupee has fallen so much; in dollar terms, the story is much flatter.

Where gold disappoints

The first disappointment is the cost of owning it. Jewellery carries making charges that are never recovered. Bars carry premiums and storage costs. Every sale involves a spread. The second disappointment is tax and paperwork: large gold holdings are hard to document, hard to insure and hard to move. The third is opportunity cost — money in gold is money not in a business, an education or a productive asset that pays you while you hold it.

How much gold makes sense

The standard advice from portfolio theory is a modest allocation — often quoted as 5 to 10% — held as a diversifier, not as the main event. In Pakistan the honest answer is more personal: many families already hold far more than 10% of their wealth in gold jewellery, often without counting it. If that is you, the next rupee of savings is probably better placed somewhere else — a savings account, a government savings scheme, or your children's education. Gold's role is to protect a portion of wealth, not to be the whole of it.

The bottom line

Gold is a good servant and a bad master. As a hedge against rupee depreciation, held in bars or coins at a modest share of your wealth, it does its job quietly and well. As the only savings plan, or bought as jewellery at full making charges and counted as an investment, it disappoints. Buy it for what it is, know what it costs, and keep the receipt.
